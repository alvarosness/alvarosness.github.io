What Is Learning?

Learning is the acquisition of knowledge and skills.
All new learning requires a foundation of prior knowledge, so in some way, learning is an iterative process that relies on making connections to existing knowledge and experiences.

Learning is better when it is harder, for it yields better mastery of the topic and longer retention compared to when it's made "easy".

The book Make It Stick talks in depth about learning and is where most of the concepts in this and related notes originate from.

You learn better when you go wide (i.e. BFS), drawing on all of your aptitudes and resourcefulness

Learning Building Blocks

All forms and practices of learning are built atop one or more of the following concepts, hence them being coined building blocks.

  • Encoding
  • Consolidation
  • Retrieval Practice
  • Generation effect
  • Elaboration
  • Spaced repetition
  • Abstraction

These building blocks are incorporated into Learning patterns that reliably lead to long lasting learning.

Note that there are Learning anti-patterns that degrade the learning experience and yield subpar results given the time it takes to execute on them.

How Learning Occurs

The learning process roughly follows these steps:

  1. Encoding - the process of converting sensory perceptions into mental representations of observed patterns called memory traces
  2. Consolidation - the process of strengthening memory traces and storing them in long term memory
  3. Retrieval - the act of remembering memory traces stored in long term memory

For learning to be durable, in addition to the step-by-step process described prior, one needs to associate the new material with a diverse set of cues that will help in recalling that knowledge. This is often done in the form of relating to prior knowledge (hence why new learning requires prior knowledge).

This is self-aggrandizing but I was able to do this instinctively when I was younger (was I a savant like that?)

Knowledge, skills, and experiences that are vivid and hold significance, and those that are periodically practiced, stay with us.

Embracing Difficulty

When learning is difficult it yields better understanding and remembering. One should embrace the difficulties that come with learning and be patient with how apparently "slow" the learning becomes compared to other methods that seem easy but give you an Illusion of mastery. That learning will yield results in the short term that seem very desirable but in actuality, does not last long.

The old adage To go fast, first go slow applies here. Difficulty forces us to slow down and truly internalize what is being learned or trained (e.g. Mia Blundetto's PLF practice).

Rather than having an illusion of master, it's best to have Confidence based on repeated performance. And that can be achieved by learning with an embrace for the difficulty that comes with it.

However, not all difficulty in learning is the same. There's desirable and undesirable difficulties

Desirable Difficulties

Reconsolidation of Memory

During a focused, effortful recall, the learning is made pliable again: the most salient aspects of it become clearer, and the consequent reconsolidation helps to reinforce meaning, strengthen connections to prior knowledge, bolster the cues and retrieval routes for recalling it later, and weaken competing routes.

Creation of Mental Models

Mental Models are forms of deeply entrenched and highly efficient skills or knowledge structures that, like habits, can be adapted and applied in varied circumstances.
And doing these is rather hard but it provides significant benefits in the long run.

Variability

Retrieval difficulties posed by spacing, interleaving, and variation are overcome by invoking the same mental processes that will be needed when applying the learning to practice.
